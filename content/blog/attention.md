# Attention is all you need, and attention is all you’ve lost

Watching my 16-year-old brother on his devices, I’m often amazed by how effortlessly his generation multitasks, like they can do several things at once, constantly switching screens, weaving in and out of interfaces as if they’re literally surfing: gaming while drawing, or scrolling through short videos while cooking. Come to think of it, we all multitask our way through every single day. Conventional wisdom says this is a terrible habit, that it damages our brains and leaves our attention drifting from one focal point to the next. Looked at another way, though, our brains are more than capable of handling it, but the cost is just giving up sustained focus on any single subject.

By that logic, returning to a one-on-one window should bring our focus back. However, every time I use AI tools lately, I find myself frustrated by their dialogue-based interface. 

Engaging in a super lengthy conversation with a single partner on a single topic is actually a deeply counter-intuitive way to communicate in real life, which is extremely linear. Inside it, my attention falls into a strange paradox: hyper-focused and weirdly scattered at the exact same time. Especially when a prompt comes back with a wall of text, I often slip into a sleepwalking haze halfway through reading, completely forgetting the center of gravity of my original question. This kind of exchange has no real finish line, yet LLMs love to tack a forced little wrap-up onto the end of every single reply. After a long session, I’m often left in the daze of waking from a heavy dream, convinced that we just stumbled onto something genuinely meaningful,but as soon as I go back and look through the endless streams of text, all I see are meaningless breaks, and fail to pinpoint the exact line where it happened. It’s ridiculously easy to lose your bearings.

In real life, when people actually want to dig deep into a question, they rarely do it in a text-based chat window with this back-and-forth exchange. For me at least, messaging apps are meant for tossing around fragmented thoughts or sending quick heads-ups. Besides, everyday texting is naturally out of sync: while the other person is still typing their next line, I’ve already fired off a reply to the point before; once they finish sending a long paragraph, they’ll circle back to pick up what I just threw out. Human memory is remarkably elastic. Even when topics jump around constantly - to the point where scrolling back through a chat with a friend yields zero 'main idea' — our brains quietly catch those continuous signals and piece them together in the background. Even when the intel arrives in scattered fragments, we still walk away able to reconstruct the full arc of a piece of gossip from start to finish.

Ironically, every time we chat with an AI in a window, the system automatically slaps a summary title onto the thread, even though that label may miss the mark. And when we try to go deep inside that one-on-one text box, the AI’s habit of absorbing the context wholesale and pushing forward along a single linear track ends up blurring our focus amid the sheer volume of words.

So I’m increasingly convinced that what truly hinders our ability to think deeply isn’t the cognitive load caused by switching between tasks, but rather the inertia of linear conversation, which leads us to passively absorb excessive and redundant information and cuts off deep thought before it even has a chance to begin. In an interaction like this, attention isn’t distracted, it’s depleted. And once it’s drained, our minds can no longer naturally grow a systematic mind map in the background the way they do in everyday conversation.

Sure, attention is all you need, but attention has always been able to inhabit multiple branches at once. Fragmented narrative isn’t the enemy of deep thought, on the contrary, I see it as vital to sparking creativity. There’s nothing inherently wrong with a linear workflow, which plays a crucial role in boosting efficiency. Yet the moment we get pulled into it passively, we may look more focused on the surface while actually losing far more attention underneath. Between 'heightened efficiency' and 'getting lost in overload,' the real dividing line is a sense of agency, a degree of intentional planning. Since an AI can’t riff out of sync with us the way a human friend intuitively does, we need to step into a session with deliberate intent if we want to take in massive amounts of information without losing the thread of our own thinking.

Working within today’s standard AI chat framework, I think two changes could be implemented right away:

First: a live mind map that grows alongside the conversation: externalizing the hidden structure in the background to counter the disorientation of linear scrolling.

Second: the interrupt and truncate mechanics in linear reading: when reading a long AI response, you should be able to jump in at the very first anchor point that sparks a reaction and carry the conversation forward from there. More importantly, once I interject with a new thought halfway through a paragraph, the AI’s 'memory' of its own previous reply in subsequent turns should cut off strictly right before I jumped in, treating the unread second half as if it had never happened.

There’s a bit of dark comedy to it when you think about it: the foundational architecture behind every large model today comes from that famous paper, 'Attention Is All You Need.' While machines have grown endlessly capable through attention mechanisms, those of us on this side of the screen are slowly burning through our own attention inside their never-ending chat boxes. Good interaction shouldn’t hypnotize us down a one-way street with exhaustive walls of text, it should leave room for branching off, going off-script, and cutting in mid-sentence. After all, whether it’s trading out-of-sync gossip with a friend or surfing across interfaces like my 16-year-old brother, the most captivating thing about human thought has always been our ability to wander off the main thread, and then still know exactly where we’re going.
